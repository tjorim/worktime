Where the interface gets in the way

Nineteen findings from driving the running app, each traced to the code that produces it. The app is well built — accessible markup, a documented contrast palette, real empty states. What follows is where that care hasn't propagated evenly.

CAL-1#1125

The month calendar throws away the shift color language

High

Every day cell renders text-bg-success when working and text-bg-secondary when off, so morning, evening and night are one undifferentiated green. Meanwhile Current Status shows tonight's shift as a purple "Night" badge and the Schedule tab shows M blue / E red / N purple. The calendar is the default tab, so the first thing a user sees contradicts everywhere else — and the whole point of a month view is scanning the rotation pattern at a glance, which the single green makes impossible.

Pass the full ShiftResult["shift"] into DayCell instead of {code, label, isWorking}, and render the existing <ShiftBadge>, which already applies shift.className and carries the tooltip.

Layout and density

The app spends its most valuable pixels — the first screen — on the same block every time.

LAY-1#1126

The status hero occupies the entire first screen, on every tab

High

Current Status is rendered by HomePage above the tab bar, so it shows identically whether you're looking at the calendar or at Gantt. It costs about 570 px on desktop; on a 390×844 phone it consumes 100% of the viewport, so every visit to every tab starts with a scroll before any tab content is visible. On Time Off, Time Tracking and Gantt the shift status isn't even relevant to the task at hand.

Collapse it to a single-line summary strip (Team 3 · Night 23:00–07:00 · in 16h 32m) that expands on tap, or render the full card only on the Calendar and Schedule tabs. A sticky one-line strip would also make the status visible while scrolled down, which it currently isn't.
frontend/src/pages/HomePage.tsx components/CurrentStatus.tsx
LAY-2#1127

Calendar cells reserve 140 px for a 24 px badge

High

.month-calendar-day sets min-height: 140px (110 px under 768 px). A typical cell holds a day number and one shift badge — roughly 50 px of content — so about two-thirds of every cell is empty. Six rows produce an 840 px grid that could be 400 px, meaning a full month never fits on one screen and the rotation pattern has to be reconstructed from memory while scrolling.

Drop the floor to roughly 4.5 rem and let the row grow only when a day actually carries holiday, time-off and payday markers. A "compact / comfortable" density toggle would serve both the scanner and the annotator.
frontend/src/styles/_calendar.scss:58 _calendar.scss:454
LAY-3#1126

"Today" and "Up Next" say the same thing twice

Medium

When the next shift is today's shift — the common case for anyone not mid-shift — the two side-by-side panels both read "Night, 23:00–07:00". Half the status card's width carries no new information, and the duplication makes the reader stop and check whether they've misread something.

When "Up Next" resolves to today's shift, drop the second panel and let the Today panel take the full width; show "Up Next" only when it's a genuinely different day, and label it with that day.

Navigation and information architecture

Getting to the right place, and knowing where you are.

NAV-1#1128

Mobile tabs are icon-only, and four of the six icons are calendars

High

Below 576 px .main-tab-label is hidden, leaving six 16 px glyphs. Four of them are calendar variants — bi-calendar3, bi-calendar-range, bi-calendar-week, bi-calendar-check — for Calendar, Unified, Schedule and Time Off. At phone size they are effectively indistinguishable, so the primary navigation becomes trial and error. The aria-labels are correct, which means screen-reader users are better served here than sighted ones.

Keep short labels under the icons (a two-line tab is still under 56 px), or move to a bottom tab bar with labels — the standard pattern for an installed PWA, and it puts navigation in thumb reach. Failing that, choose visually distinct glyphs: a grid, a list, a person, a check.
frontend/src/styles/_utilities.scss components/MainTabs.tsx
NAV-2#1128

"Calendar" and "Unified" don't tell you which calendar to open

Medium

Two adjacent tabs are both calendars, distinguished by a word that describes the implementation (one view unifies several data sources) rather than what the user gets. A third tab, "Schedule", is also a calendar.

Name them by content — "My month" and "Everything" — or fold the unified view in as a display mode of the main calendar rather than a sibling tab.

SET-2#1129

Two sidebar sections are named after nothing in particular

Medium

"Quick Actions" is the backup, restore and reset section; "Information" is About. Neither label predicts its contents, so finding "export my data" means opening sections at random. Team and schedule selection also live under "Preferences", which is where a user looking to change teams is least likely to check.

Rename to "Data & backup" and "About". Consider promoting Schedule & team to its own section, since it's the highest-consequence setting in the app.

Flows and feedback

What happens when a control can't be used, or there's nothing there yet.

FLW-1#1130

The two primary time-tracking buttons are disabled without saying why

Medium

"Start Now" and "Add Task" render as pale filled buttons — on mobile, full-width and reading as enabled — and are disabled until the form validates. Nothing states the condition. Worse, the Label field's only guidance is the placeholder "Add labels first", a dead end with no way to add one from there. GanttTaskModal and TemplateModal both render tt_add_labels_first_help() under the same field; the daily entry form, the one people use every day, doesn't.

Add tt_add_labels_first_help() to TaskEntryForm with an inline "Create a label" action, and give each disabled button an aria-describedby hint naming the missing field.
components/timeTracking/TaskEntryForm.tsx:114 TaskEntryForm.tsx:171 TaskEntryForm.tsx:179
FLW-2#1130

Empty states ship with a full toolbar of impossible actions

Medium

With no time-off events, Time Off still shows "Delete Selected", "Select All" and "Clear Selection" (all disabled) plus the hint "Select events from the table to edit or delete" — above a table that doesn't exist. The one button that matters, "Add Event", sits on a second row beneath Import/Export.

Show only "Add Event" and "Import" until the first event exists; reveal the selection toolbar when a row is actually selected.
components/TimeOffView.tsx components/timeOff/TimeOffToolbar.tsx
FLW-3#1131

Onboarding announces eight steps before showing any value

Medium

Step 1 opens with "Step 1 of 8 / 12.5% Complete" — a commitment ask before the user knows whether the app is for them. Only two of the eight steps (schedule, team) are needed to make the app work; the rest configure features that are off by default. The step also repeats "Welcome to Worktime!" as both modal title and body heading, states progress three ways at once, and styles "Maybe Later" as a solid gray filled button that competes visually with the primary.

Cut the required flow to schedule and team, and prompt for each feature's setup the first time it's enabled. Keep the progress bar, drop the redundant "Step X of Y" and percentage, and make "Maybe Later" a text link.
components/WelcomeWizard.tsx components/wizardStepConfig.ts
FLW-4#1134

Nothing tells the user they're offline

Low

navigator.onLine is read inside useOngoingSync to skip a flush, but no UI reflects it. An offline edit looks identical to a synced one, and the sync indicator is hidden entirely for unauthenticated users. For an installed PWA aimed at shift workers — often on site with poor signal — "you're offline, 3 changes queued" is worth surfacing.

Add an offline tone to SyncStatusIndicator driven by the online/offline window events, above the existing error case in the priority list.

Theming and platform

The dark palette is documented carefully for contrast, less so for hierarchy.

THM-1#1132

Dark mode flattens the card hierarchy

Medium

Body sits at #181a1b and cards at #23272b — a difference too small to read as elevation, so the nested structure that's obvious in light mode (page → status card → Today/Up Next panels) collapses into one dark field held together by hairline borders. Light mode gets white cards on gray; dark mode gets near-black on near-black.

Widen the step between body, card and nested card, and add a subtle top highlight or shadow so elevation survives. Aim for the same perceived separation the light theme has.
styles/main.scss styles/_variables.scss
THM-2#1132

The navbar stays full-saturation blue in dark mode

Low

The header keeps bg="primary" (#0d6efd) in both themes, so a bright, fully saturated band sits across the top of a near-black page. For an app whose users are looking at night shifts at 3 a.m., that's the one surface worth toning down.

Give the navbar a dark-theme surface token — a deep desaturated blue against the near-black ground — rather than reusing the light-theme primary.
components/Header.tsx
THM-3#270

Reduced-motion is honored in exactly one place

Low

prefers-reduced-motion guards the sync spinner and nothing else. Day-cell transitions, modal and toast animations, and tab transitions all still run. Issue #270 already tracks the broader accessibility work.

Add one global reduced-motion block that neutralizes transitions and animations, rather than guarding each animation individually.

What's already good

Worth saying, because these are the parts not to disturb: a skip link and aria-keyshortcuts in the header; shift badge contrast ratios documented inline with the reasoning for each dark-mode override; toasts that pause their autohide on hover and focus so an Undo stays reachable; a shared EmptyState component used consistently; assertive versus polite announcement chosen per toast severity; lazy-loaded tab content with a labeled loading state. The gaps above are unevenness, not absence.
